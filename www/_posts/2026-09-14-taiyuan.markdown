We live in Heidelberg, Germany. About once a year we travel to my wife's hometown of Taiyuan, the capital of the Shanxi province in China, to visit my parents-in-law. Our most recent visit was in spring of this year, for two months. In this post I'll talk about my experiences of regularly staying in Taiyuan since 2019.

## Getting There

Currently Germans can stay for 30 days in China without a visa. Since we stayed in Mainland China for the entire two months, we couldn't make use of it this year unfortunately. Last year we did a quick trip to Hong Kong, so we had two separate 30-day periods.

Luckily we live pretty close to the Frankfurt airport, which has direct flights to Beijing using Air China:

[![Air China plane from Frankfurt to Beijing](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0505_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0505.jpg)

From Beijing airport we cross the city by subway to the train station, which is slightly challenging with a stroller and heavy luggage:

[![Beijing subway map](/public/taiyuan/subway_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/subway.jpg)

From there it's a 2.5 hour trip by high speed train, recently upgraded to 350 km/h. Since the trains are pretty cheap and convenient they are quite popular, so you have to book a ticket early for popular times and fast trains:

  <source src="/public/taiyuan/trainstation.mp4" type="video/mp4">
</video>

I haven't seen a single train being delayed so far, which is refreshing after having ridden trains regularly for about 15 years in delay-plagued Germany. The high speed rail network of China is amazing in general, with more high speed tracks existing inside of China than outside:

## City

The Taiyuan area is famous for coal and steel, similarly to the Ruhr area in Germany. You can still see a lot of industry, including a [huge steel production facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiyuan_Iron_and_Steel_Group) in our backyard and coal refinement:

[![Coal refining](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7801_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7801.jpg)

Solar has seen a big boom in China, here's a random hill covered in solar cells:

[![Solar cells on mountain](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0164_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0164.jpg)

Taiyuan boasts a population of 4-5 million, making it a similar size to Berlin. Yet it seems to be widely unknown, at least I had never heard of it before meeting my wife. There are a lot of huge cities in China after all. While China itself is not growing in population anymore, but shrinking, people from villages and smaller cities are moving to larger cities, so Taiyuan is still growing at ~1.7% per year. Compare that to Heidelberg's measly ~0.4% yearly growth.

[![Taiyuan from above](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9652_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9652.jpg)

Shanxi is not at China's coast, but a bit inland. This has cut it off from a lot of the recent boom China's coastal cities have received thanks to access to international shipping. Historically Taiyuan played a much larger role in China and was the capital of many dynasties. Unfortunately following the Cultural Revolution there isn't much left of historic buildings. What looks like historic buildings in the center of Taiyuan often turns out to be replicas:

[![Shopping street](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8501_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8501.jpg)

Nevertheless Taiyuan has some actual ancient sights like the [Jinci temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinci) and [Twin Pagoda Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_Pagoda_Temple). [Pingyao Ancient City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pingyao) is also only 30 minutes by train.

There doesn't seem to be a lot of tourism or international travel to Taiyuan in general. This year I've seen a handful of people from outside of East Asia, in the years before it was even less. When I looked for some information on the city, I couldn't find much in English online, and what I could find wasn't [that kind](https://www.reddit.com/r/China/comments/8sqw6j/thoughts_on_taiyuan/e11w93t/). I can imagine that life in the villages is indeed less interesting:

[![Village](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7750_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7750.jpg)

## Pollution

While we're talking about the bad sides, air pollution can be tough, especially during the winter. Since I don't enjoy cycling in -20°C either, I'm now avoiding going to Taiyuan in winter:

[![Winter](/public/taiyuan/IMG_20190202_145510_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_20190202_145510.jpg)

In recent years the pollution has been getting better though, spring is usually dry with nice weather. Occasionally there are dust storms from the Gobi desert, which are unpleasant to be in, and about the only time you really need an air purifier at home. But on most days you can see far and the air seems fine:

[![Clear views](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0197_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0197.jpg)

## Fen River

The Fen river (or Fenhe, literally "Large river") that runs through the city is unfortunately quite polluted, so swimming in it is not advised. The waterfront has been made into a huge park of sorts, with walking paths and cycling paths along the river for the main part of the city. This allows crossing through the city from north to south or vice versa undisturbed by cars or scooters:

[![View of re-naturized area around Fen river in the north](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6798_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6798.jpg)

Interestingly the Fen river doesn't carry enough water naturally. Taiyuan has also been plagued by the entire city sinking by more than 2 meters since 1949 from the groundwater lowering by about 70 meters, mostly caused by intensive agriculture, coal mining and heavy industry. This trend has recently been [partially reversed](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2021/dec/01/terrawatch-what-the-world-can-learn-from-chinas-sinking-city). Planting trees and reducing water usage seem to have helped, but the major driver has been the [Wanjiazhai Water Control Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wanjiazhai_Dam), which redirects over a billion cubic meters of water per year from the Yellow river to the Fen river.

[![Bridge crossing Fen river](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6778_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6778.jpg)

China seems to have a thing for huge infrastructure projects, we've already talked a bit about the high speed railway network, you'll also see some nice looking bridges later.

[![Fen river in the south](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8140_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8140.jpg)

Some older men seem not to be disturbed by the river's pollution. During runs I've quickly learnt where the hot spots of naked swimming are (and to avoid them). There is a nice looking public pool next to the river, so you can still safely swim.

## Traffic & Transportation

Car traffic during rush hour seems bad, but luckily we rarely had to go through it. The experience of staying in a city during vacation isn't representative of actually living and working there after all. On smaller roads there are more electric scooters than cars. [Delivery drivers](https://www.scmp.com/tech/article/3357458/china-moves-end-irrational-food-delivery-subsidies-and-sectors-price-wars) make up a huge fraction of the e-scooters. Gasoline-powered scooters are heavily restricted in the inner city.

[![Road at night](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7612_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7612.jpg)

Street signs include a N/E/S/W designation to show which direction the street is going.

[![Street sign at a park](/public/taiyuan/83c7d19942b61164480395fb74d53f1e_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/83c7d19942b61164480395fb74d53f1e.jpg)

With a stroller taking a bus is more convenient than using bicycles. It is also extremely cheap at 0.5 RMB per ride, or about 0.06 €. This is literally the only photo/video I found including a bus, featuring some bonus sheep coming down from the mountains:

Two subway lines have been opened in the city so far, one going from North to South (opened in 2020), the other going to the airport (opened in 2025).

[![Subway station](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8446_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_8446.jpg)

For navigation [Amap](https://www.amap.com/) works great, outside the city I've also been relying on [CoMaps](https://www.comaps.app/). Unfortunately Amap only shows exact arrival times at bus stations when set to Chinese, not in English. Luckily searching still works with Pinyin (romanization system) even when the UI is set to be in Chinese.

The bus and subway stop running pretty early in the evening, so we had to also use ridesharing. Didi and Amap are the usual solutions and cost about 1-5 € for our usual trips through the city.

There is a [(barely-international) airport in Taiyuan](https://www.flightconnections.com/flights-from-taiyuan-tyn), which mostly connects to other Chinese cities, including Hong Kong and Macau. Last year there also were direct flights to Thailand, but they don't seem to exist anymore.

## Restrictions and the Internet

For using the internet as a foreigner, relying on Google, WhatsApp, Wikipedia, etc., the [Great Firewall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Firewall) is annoying, but using a cheap Hong Kong eSIM from 3HK easily circumvents it. My interpretation is that China is only trying to prevent their own citizens from accessing the wider internet and doesn't care about visitors.

During the evening connections to Europe are noticeably slower, I guess the total bandwidth for traffic outside of China is just pretty limited. Chinese people might not mind much since they mostly use local services anyway.

For payment Alipay and WeChat Pay work well, and both are literally accepted and preferred by anyone, including the smallest street food shops. It can be connected to a credit card and at least for me the credit card fees were waived every time for some reason. You have to upload your passport in the app to verify your identity, which is slightly disconcerting, but seems to become more common in Western apps nowadays too. You can still get around with cash, but shop owners might scramble to get change from a colleague.

[![Outdoor restaurant](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7296_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7296.jpg)

The [Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taiyuan_Satellite_Launch_Center) has been in operation since 1966. It's located about 300 km from the city and has [regular launches](https://orbitalradar.com/launch-database/site/taiyuan). Unfortunately watching them seems not to be allowed, especially for foreigners.

[![Far view](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9632_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9632.jpg)

## Language, School & Work

My Chinese is very basic, so I have trouble getting around. Unfortunately most people in Taiyuan don't speak any English. I have noticed that school-age kids would mostly just say rote phrases in English, but not actually be able to communicate with me.

School and work schedules seem crazy to me, with both kids and adults staying at school/work all day. I can't imagine learning or working for that many hours in the day, my brain would just shut off after a few hours and I'd have to switch to something lighter.

There seems to be a huge difference in the quality of schools, to the point where some famous public schools would take over other public schools to lend them some of their prestige. Parents move (or pretend to move) to an area to give their children access to a good school. The craziest traffic I've seen is in front of a school, with scooters, people and cars bringing the entire street to a stop during pickup times. I couldn't even get through on foot.

There is also barely any time off, so when weekend or holidays hit all the nice spots are overcrowded. See the parking traffic at Forest Park for example:

[![Traffic at Forest Park](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6113_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6113.jpg)

## Food

The food in Taiyuan is amazing. I like noodle dishes and that's what Shanxi is famous for. The northern part of China is way more about wheat and millet, which is what they've been growing for thousands of years.

[![Shanxi Hot Pot](/public/taiyuan/hotpot_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/hotpot.jpg)

But you're not restricted to local dishes of course. You can have all kinds of East Asian cuisines, including the full variety of Chinese regions. There are so many restaurants that on a single street you'll find more variety of foods than in all of Heidelberg.

[![Various meals](/public/taiyuan/restaurant_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/restaurant.jpg)

Prices are pretty low at restaurants, a normal dinner would set us back 3-8 € per adult.

[![Spicy bowl](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9390_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9390.jpg)

People regularly bring their own drinks, for example bubble tea or alcohol, or even food from another restaurant, to the restaurant they're eating at:

[![Barbecue with outside seating](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0201_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_0201.jpg)

My favorite is eating street food instead of sitting down in a restaurant. A few years ago most of the improvised street food stalls were banned from the city, but they seem to have made a comeback. I seem not to be alone in liking them, since they are pretty crowded on evenings and during the weekend. The highlight is lamb skewers for 0.25 - 0.5 € each:

[![Restaurant without sitting, dedicated to skewers](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9935_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9935.jpg)

[![Lamb skewers](/public/taiyuan/skewers_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/skewers.jpg)

The "Food Street" of Taiyuan features fake Ming/Qing dynasty style buildings and has lots of street food available. But there are also smaller hot spots around the city. Just taking a bus crossing through the city in the evening you'll run past lots of places where people are sitting/standing outside eating street food.

[![Another popular street food spot](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7357_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_7357.jpg)

In recent years [Mixue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixue_Ice_Cream_%26_Tea) has gotten extremely popular. You can now find the shops on many street corners, selling ice cream (0.25 €), lemonade (0.5 €) and bubble tea (1 €). Quoting Wikipedia:

> At the end of 2025, it operated almost 60,000 stores in China and overseas, making it the world's largest food-service chain by number of stores.

[![Mixue](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6752_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_6752.jpg)

## Conclusion

I feel like I've been extremely lucky to get to spend months every year in Taiyuan, which I now consider a hidden gem. It has easy access to my favorite foods, great running paths and mountain roads for cycling:

[![Me at the 2nd Fen Reservoir](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9619_small.jpg)](/public/taiyuan/IMG_9619.jpg)
